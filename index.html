Hotter Summers, Bigger Bills

By the time today’s college students retire, how many more days will their city need air conditioning?

Shaun Israni · Corey Ngo · Abhinav Krishna · Srinivas Sriram

Scroll to find out ↓

Climate change usually arrives as one global number, like “+2.7 °C by 2100.” It is hard to feel a global average. But everyone feels a summer power bill. So we translated climate-model temperature projections (CMIP6) into cooling-degree-days (CDD), the number utilities use to forecast air-conditioning demand. Every degree that a day’s average temperature sits above 65 °F adds one CDD. A city with 3,000 CDD a year needs roughly three times as much cooling as one with 1,000.

The takeaway

The places that will feel hotter summers the most are the ones least built for them.

Phoenix and Miami will add the most cooling-degree-days, but they already have air conditioning everywhere. Minneapolis, Chicago and New York will see their cooling demand double to quadruple, in cities where many homes, schools and apartments were never built to be cooled. Seeing every city on one map hides that; putting absolute and relative change side by side, and following one city through three futures, makes it obvious. And the gap between the scenarios shows this is still a choice: under strong climate action, cooling demand levels off by mid-century instead of climbing all the way to 2100.

Data & methods

  • Source: CMIP6 climate model output from the NOAA/Pangeo public dataset on Google Cloud (gs://cmip6): monthly near-surface air temperature (Amon/tas, member r1i1p1f1).
  • Models: GFDL-ESM4, MPI-ESM1-2-LR and MRI-ESM2-0. Lines show the three-model mean unless noted.
  • Scenarios: historical (1985–2014) and SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP5-8.5 (2015–2100).
  • Cooling-degree-days: for the grid cell nearest each city, days in month × max(0, monthly mean °F − 65), summed per year. Trend lines use a 10-year rolling mean.
  • Limitations: monthly means and ~1° grid cells smooth out hot days, so absolute CDD run below station-based normals (Phoenix ≈3,100 here vs ≈4,500 observed) and coastal Seattle reads near zero (excluded from ratios). Relative changes and directions are robust. The bill estimate holds electricity prices and home efficiency constant.
